\section{Theoretical Reflection}\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{AI and Work: Augmentation, Automation, and the Jagged Frontier}

\subsubsection{The Jagged Frontier in Practice}

Dell'Acqua et al.\parencite{DellAcqua2026} introduce the ``jagged technological frontier'' to describe how AI assistance improves performance on some tasks while degrading it on others, even within the same knowledge workflow and at similar perceived difficulty. Our build process is a case study in navigating this frontier.

\textbf{Inside the frontier} (AI helped):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Boilerplate Python class definitions, docstrings, and type hints.
    \item LaTeX equation formatting and notation tables.
    \item Multi-language UI translation (200+ terms from Chinese to pharmaceutical-logistics English).
    \item CSS animation and responsive layout code.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Outside the frontier} (AI harmed or misled):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dependency management for Cloud deployment: the AI added \texttt{torchvision} to solve a symptom, not the cause.
    \item Reward function tuning: initial $\alpha_{\text{setup}} = 5.0$ produced too many small waves; the AI did not recognize the behavioral pattern without human specification.
    \item Product positioning: the AI's natural tendency was to add features (Pro edition with NSGA-II, federated learning, 3D visualizations). Left unchecked, this would have produced a bloated product with no clear entry point.
\end{itemize}

The last point connects directly to Shipper's argument in ``After Automation''\parencite{shipper2026after}. Shipper observes that AI commoditizes the ``residue of human expertise---whatever can be made explicit enough to train on.'' This collapses the value of default model output and creates demand for what is different. In our case, the AI could generate feature after feature, but it could not determine which features Wang Li would actually pay for. That judgment---\textit{what is scarce when execution is cheap}\parencite{shipper2026after}---remained human.

\subsubsection{Knowledge Codification and Its Risks}

Jurowetzki\parencite{Jurowetzki2026}, building on Cowan et al.\parencite{cowan2000codification}, decomposes codification into four capacities: encoding, evaluation, maintenance, and revision. AI dramatically lowers encoding costs (writing code, generating documentation), but it raises maintenance and revision costs in subtle ways.

Consider our \texttt{requirements.txt}. The AI encoded dependencies quickly. But when the Cloud environment changed underneath us---when \texttt{transformers} introduced a new internal scan behavior, when \texttt{torchvision}'s version pinning shifted---\textit{who evaluated and revised the encoded knowledge}? The answer was: the human, because the AI had no model of Streamlit Cloud's container behavior.

This generates what Jurowetzki calls \textbf{orphaned codification}: artifacts whose premises no one can contest. Our codebase contains 4,000+ lines of AI-generated Streamlit dashboard code. If Streamlit 2.0 deprecates \texttt{st.dataframe} or changes its theming API, who on our team would notice? The person who originally specified the UI layout---but that person is not necessarily the one who can trace through 1,700 lines of generated CSS.

\subsubsection{Augmentation vs. Automation}

Raisch and Krakowski\parencite{raisch2021automation} frame the ``automation-augmentation paradox'': AI systems that automate routine tasks may simultaneously create demand for higher-level human judgment. Maverick-SORT is designed as an augmentation tool, not an automation replacement. Wang Li remains the decision-maker; the system recommends but does not execute. The What-If simulator is deliberately named a ``simulator,'' not an ``optimizer,'' to signal that the human must still choose.

This design choice has a cost: it limits the system's immediate efficiency gains. A full automation integration could push wave decisions directly to pickers' handheld devices. But it also limits adoption risk: operation managers do not need to trust a black box; they need to trust a transparent recommendation they can override.

\subsection{Adoption and Diffusion: A TOE Framework Perspective}

The Technology--Organization--Environment (TOE) framework provides a useful lens for understanding whether Maverick-SORT can move beyond a prototype and reach real operational use.

\paragraph{Technology.}
Maverick-SORT is designed as a decision-support layer rather than a replacement for existing WMS platforms. While WMS systems excel at transaction processing and execution, they provide limited support for simulation, optimization, and scenario analysis. By remaining standalone and browser-based, the system delivers predictive intelligence without requiring costly modifications to legacy infrastructure.

\paragraph{Organization.}
The key adopter is not the IT department but the Operations Director. Unlike IT managers, whose priority is system stability and risk avoidance, operations managers are directly responsible for warehouse efficiency and service performance. The Essential Edition lowers adoption barriers by allowing them to test potential value independently through a zero-deployment ``What-If'' environment.

\paragraph{Environment.}
Pharmaceutical logistics operates under strict GSP compliance requirements. Rather than treating regulation as an obstacle, Maverick-SORT uses explainable, knowledge-guided recommendations to align optimization with existing operational rules. Regulatory pressure therefore becomes a driver of adoption rather than a barrier.

\paragraph{The Most Limiting Condition.}
Among all TOE factors, the greatest constraint is organizational champion bandwidth. Operations managers often lack the time, attention, and political capital required to evaluate new technologies. As a result, adoption depends less on algorithmic performance than on reducing the effort required to explore the solution. The Essential Edition, free trial, and five-minute simulation workflow are all designed to minimize this activation cost.

\subsection{What We Learned from Demo Day}

The recent class Demo Day provided a harsh but necessary reality check regarding how the system is perceived versus how it was built. The live feedback highlighted several critical insights that directly reshaped our product positioning:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{What resonated most (The Architecture)}: The audience was highly engaged by the systemic relationships between different features---specifically how the underlying KGDRL algorithm directly fed into the user-facing ``Algorithm Arena'' and the AI Copilot explanations.
    \item \textbf{What was ignored (The Visual Clutter)}: The complex data visualization dashboards based on live CSV uploads received almost no attention. Without sufficient presentation time to explain their specific operational contexts, they simply appeared as generic charts. This proved a harsh lesson: visual complexity does not inherently translate to perceived business value.
    \item \textbf{The core skepticism (Product Identity)}: The most piercing critiques challenged the product's fundamental identity. Because the initial presentation was a generic ``feature walkthrough,'' evaluators rightly questioned: \textit{``What is the actual core value? What is the single selling point?''}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The Pivot (Shifting the Adoption Barrier)}: This skepticism revealed that our immediate adoption barrier was not technical, but narrative. This feedback directly drove the fundamental restructuring of Section 1 in this report. We abandoned the ``feature-list'' approach and optimized the product positioning into the ``Risk Reversal Engine.'' We learned that before a user cares about advanced data dashboards (the Pro Edition), they must first be convinced by a singular, zero-risk ROI validation (the Essential Edition).
